# Natural Farming: Core Principles

Natural farming (Japanese *shizen nōhō*), associated with Masanobu Fukuoka, is a low-intervention approach that works with natural processes rather than overriding them. It rests on four working principles:

1. **No tillage.** The soil is not plowed or turned. Structure is maintained by living roots, earthworms, and soil biota rather than by mechanical cultivation.
2. **No chemical fertilizer.** Fertility is sustained by returning crop residues to the field, growing leguminous ground cover (such as white clover), and letting nutrients cycle in place.
3. **No weeding by cultivation or herbicide.** Weeds are suppressed rather than eliminated — through permanent ground cover, straw mulch, and timing sowing so crops establish ahead of weeds.
4. **No reliance on chemical pesticides.** Pest and disease pressure is held in balance by biodiversity and healthy plants instead of routine spraying.

A signature technique is **returning the straw uncut to the field** after harvest, spread over the surface to feed soil life and shelter the next crop. Seed is often sown by broadcasting **clay seed balls** (*nendo dango*) — seed coated in a mix of clay and compost — which protect it from birds and drought until rain triggers germination.

The underlying stance is observational: understand how a site's natural systems behave before intervening, and remove inputs one at a time rather than adding them.

> These principles echo, in independent modern form, the three pillars of FAO **Conservation Agriculture**: minimal soil disturbance, permanent soil cover, and crop diversification.
